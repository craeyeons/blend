\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The role of this section is not to demonstrate that a hybrid PINN--solver
field can outperform either component in isolation; that has been
established by previous work on domain decomposition and a-posteriori
refinement \citep{jagtap2020extended,moseley2023finite}. Our aim is
narrower and more diagnostic. The framework of
Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:hybrid} rests on two empirical claims
that are not self-evident: (i) the proxy $\rho^\theta$ defined in
\eqref{eq:rho-def} actually tracks the unobserved pointwise PINN error
closely enough that minimising the surrogate \eqref{eq:surrogate} steers
the rejector toward decisions that reduce the true hybrid PDE error; and
(ii) the rejector trained at one set of geometries transfers to an
unseen geometry without re-tuning, despite the partition
$\widehat{\Omega}_C$ being a geometry-dependent object. Each experiment
below targets one of these claims; we deliberately avoid loading the
section with unrelated baselines.

We instantiate the framework on steady incompressible Navier--Stokes flow
past a cylinder at $\mathrm{Re}=100$ -- a setting in which PINNs are
known to underperform near the cylinder boundary and the wake
\citep{rao2020physics,wang2022whenpinns}. A linear-elliptic Poisson
problem with manufactured sharp features (a controlled stress test of
the proxy in isolation) is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:poisson}, with the same protocol but a different
operator. We focus the main text on the harder case.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{PINN and rejector.}
For each cylinder geometry $(x_c,y_c,r)$ we train a separate PINN
$h=\widehat{u}_\theta$ to convergence at that geometry, with parabolic
inflow $u_0=1.0$, no-slip on the cylinder, and standard outflow on the
right boundary; this isolates the rejector's task from any PINN training
issue and matches the deployment scenario of Section~\ref{sec:intro},
where the predictor is fixed and only the rejection rule is learned.
The rejector is a small convolutional network whose input channels are
the PINN field, the strong-form residual $\mathfrak{r}^\theta$, the
error-transport estimate $e^\theta$ from \eqref{eq:ete}, and a binary
geometry mask. We minimise the surrogate \eqref{eq:surrogate} with
$c(x)\equiv 1.1$ -- a constant cost in the dimensionless units of
$\rho^\theta$, since $\rho^\theta$ has median in $[1,2]$ by construction
\eqref{eq:rho-def} -- using Adam and an anisotropic total-variation
penalty $\lambda_{\mathrm{tv}}\,\TV(s)$ with $\lambda_{\mathrm{tv}}=1$.
The TV penalty is the $\Hcal_r$ regularisation discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-subdomain}: it biases $\widehat{\Omega}_C$
toward a finite union of solver-friendly connected regions and away
from the disconnected level-set geometry of the unrestricted optimum.
The PINN remains frozen throughout rejector training.

\paragraph{Hybrid pipeline and ground truth.}
At test time the hybrid is assembled by \eqref{eq:hybrid}: the rejector
is evaluated once on the active grid; the rejected interior
$\widehat{\Omega}_C\setminus\Gamma$ is solved by a standard finite-volume
incompressible Navier--Stokes scheme with Dirichlet data taken from
$h\big|_\Gamma$, while $h$ is retained on $\widehat{\Omega}_P\cup\Gamma$.
The same finite-volume solver, run on the full domain $\Omega$, provides
the reference solution $u^\star$ against which all errors are reported.
This is the strongest available ground truth: any error reported below
is between $\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{hyb}}$ (or $h$) and the very solver
that the hybrid uses internally as its fallback, so improvements cannot
be attributed to a discrepancy between two different reference codes.

\paragraph{Error metric.}
For a candidate field $u=(u_x,u_y,p)$ and the reference $u^\star=
(u_x^\star,u_y^\star,p^\star)$ on the shared discretisation grid
$\Omega_{\mathrm{grid}}\subset\Omega$ with $N=|\Omega_{\mathrm{grid}}|$
fluid cells, we report the gauge-invariant root-mean-square error
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}(u,u^\star)
  \;:=\;
  \sqrt{\,
  \frac{1}{N}\!\!\sum_{x\in\Omega_{\mathrm{grid}}}\!\!
  \Bigl[
    \bigl(u_x(x)-u_x^\star(x)\bigr)^2
    +\bigl(u_y(x)-u_y^\star(x)\bigr)^2
    +\bigl\|\nabla p(x)-\nabla p^\star(x)\bigr\|^2
  \Bigr]\,}.
  \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
Two design choices are deliberate. First, the velocity components enter
as raw differences because the boundary conditions fix the velocity
gauge (parabolic inflow, no-slip on the cylinder, outflow on the right);
no constant offset is admissible. Second, the pressure enters through
its gradient rather than its raw value, because the steady
incompressible Navier--Stokes equations determine $p$ only up to an
additive constant: $h$ and $u^\star$ may differ by a global offset that
carries no physical information, and including the raw pressure
difference would inflate the metric by a quantity the rejector can
neither see nor control. The pressure gradient is computed by
second-order central differences on $\Omega_{\mathrm{grid}}$, with
one-sided stencils on the boundary, so $\mathrm{RMSE}$ is a single
scalar in the units of velocity (the gradient term is rescaled by the
grid spacing during evaluation to share units with $u_x,u_y$). For the
coverage curves of Figure~\ref{fig:cyl-coverage} the same metric is
used, so the U-shape is not an artefact of a particular choice of
norm; for the per-axis error fields in Figure~\ref{fig:cyl-solution}
we plot the pointwise summands of \eqref{eq:rmse} separately.

\paragraph{Training and held-out geometries.}
The rejector is trained jointly on seven cylinder geometries that
bracket a held-out test triple along each axis
($x_c\in\{0.30,0.50,0.70,1.00\}$, $y_c\in\{0.30,0.40,0.50,0.80\}$,
$r\in\{0.10,0.12,0.15\}$); the held-out triple is $(0.60,0.55,0.11)$,
strictly in the interior of the bracket on every axis but absent from
the training set. Each geometry is paired with the PINN trained for
that geometry, so the rejector at test time receives a
\emph{(PINN, geometry)} pair it has not seen during its own training,
even though every component of the pair is individually well-defined.
Table~\ref{tab:cyl-train} lists the configurations.

\subsection{Held-out geometry: does the proxy track the true error?}
\label{sec:exp-cylinder}

The first claim to test is whether $\rho^\theta$ is a good enough
surrogate for the unobserved pointwise PINN error that minimising the
loss \eqref{eq:surrogate} drives down the \emph{true} hybrid error
\eqref{eq:hybrid-bound}, not just the proxy abstention loss. The
difficulty in stating this directly is that the rejector is trained on
$\rho^\theta$ but should be evaluated against $\|h-u^\star\|$, which is
exactly the quantity $\rho^\theta$ is meant to approximate. A fair
single-number comparison therefore involves the same scalar twice. The
clean separation we use instead is to sweep the rejector's decision
threshold $\tau$ over the entire score range, and at each $\tau$ to
record both quantities -- the surrogate loss \eqref{eq:surrogate}, which
the rejector \emph{can} see, and the RMSE of $\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{hyb}}$
against $u^\star$, which it \emph{cannot} -- as functions of the same
geometric observable, the coverage
$|\widehat{\Omega}_C|/|\Omega|$.

Plotting both curves against coverage on the held-out geometry produces
Figure~\ref{fig:cyl-coverage}. The surrogate loss is U-shaped by
construction: \eqref{eq:surrogate} has an accept term decreasing in
coverage (more high-$\rho^\theta$ points absorbed by the solver) and an
abstain term linear in coverage (fixed cost $c$ per rejected point), so
their sum is convex with a unique interior minimum at the operating
coverage selected by $c$. The substantive observation concerns the RMSE
curve. It is also U-shaped, and its minimum sits at essentially the same
coverage as the loss-curve minimum -- not at one of the two extremes one
might expect a priori, and not at a coverage offset from the loss
optimum. We discuss each end of the curve in turn before returning to
the co-location.

At low coverage the rejector keeps the PINN almost everywhere and the
hybrid inherits the PINN's pointwise errors near the cylinder and in
the wake, so RMSE is large and dominated by the regions
$\rho^\theta$ flags as high. As coverage grows past the level set
$\{\rho^\theta>c\}$, the solver absorbs precisely those high-$\rho^\theta$
regions and RMSE falls sharply. At high coverage the picture reverses
in a way that is structural rather than incidental. The remaining
PINN cells become spatially scattered, which forces the interface
$\Gamma$ to thread through high-error regions; the bound
\eqref{eq:hybrid-bound} amplifies the PINN error on $\Gamma$ by
$1+C_s$, and the experiment shows this term inflating RMSE from below
as coverage approaches $1$. The right-hand rise of the curve is therefore
the empirical signature of one-way Dirichlet coupling
(Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-couple}), not an instability of the rejector.

The relevant observation is the co-location of the two minima. The
surrogate loss is a function of $\rho^\theta$ and $c$ alone, both
computable from $h$ at zero ground-truth cost; the RMSE is a function of
$u^\star$, which the rejector never sees. If $\rho^\theta$ were a poor
surrogate for $\|h-u^\star\|$ -- if Assumption~\ref{ass:linearisation}
held only loosely -- the two minima could sit at substantially different
coverages, and the rejector trained against the loss would route to the
wrong volume of the domain. They sit at essentially the same coverage,
which is the empirical content of Theorem~\ref{thm:h-consistency}
combined with Assumption~\ref{ass:linearisation}: small surrogate
excess implies small \emph{true} hybrid excess, up to the linearisation
slack $\delta_{\mathrm{lin}}^\theta$, and the slack is small enough at
this PINN that the operating point selected from the loss agrees with
the operating point selected from the ground truth. We take this as the
direct empirical evidence that the proxy is doing the job the theory
asks of it.

\paragraph{Solution fields.}
At the loss-curve operating point $\tau^\star$, the hybrid restores the
boundary-layer pressure gradient and the wake separation that the PINN
under-resolves; the rejector contour overlaid on the hybrid panel
(Figure~\ref{fig:cyl-solution}) localises $\widehat{\Omega}_C$ around
the cylinder and the near-wake, exactly the regions where
$\rho^\theta$ is large, while the bulk upstream and far field remain on
the PINN. Quantitatively, the held-out RMSE drops from
$1.15\!\times\!10^{-1}$ (PINN alone) to $2.27\!\times\!10^{-2}$ (hybrid),
a factor of $5.1$ reduction, at $1.48\times$ wall-clock speedup over a
full solver run on the same grid and hardware. The rejector itself
contributes negligibly to wall-clock time: a single forward pass through
the small CNN, evaluated once per inference.

\subsection{Generalisation across geometries}
\label{sec:exp-generalisation}

The second claim is that a single rejector trained jointly across the
seven training geometries produces a working hybrid on each of them and
on the held-out triple, despite each geometry being paired with its own
PINN and despite the rejected subdomain $\widehat{\Omega}_C$ being a
different region of $\Omega$ in each case. This is what the routing
problem is for: if the rejector had to be retrained per geometry there
would be no amortisation and the framework would reduce to a-posteriori
refinement at deployment. We therefore evaluate the same trained
rejector on all eight (PINN, geometry) pairs of
Table~\ref{tab:cyl-train} -- seven seen during rejector training, one
not -- and report PINN RMSE, hybrid RMSE, the coverage selected at
$\tau^\star$, and end-to-end wall-clock for the hybrid and full solver
on identical hardware (Table~\ref{tab:cyl}).

The pattern is consistent across the table. Hybrid RMSE is several-fold
below PINN RMSE on every configuration, with no configuration
collapsing to all-PINN or all-solver behaviour; coverages cluster in the
$38\text{--}50\%$ band, indicating that the rejector consistently
identifies a non-trivial high-error subdomain rather than abstaining
uniformly; and end-to-end wall-clock is in the $1.5\text{--}1.9\times$
speedup band against full solver runs, with the held-out triple
sitting inside this band rather than degrading at the boundary of the
training bracket. The held-out row in particular shows the same
qualitative behaviour as the training rows -- comparable coverage, a
several-fold RMSE reduction, and a speedup in the same band -- which
indicates that the rejector has learned a residual--error mapping that
generalises across the training bracket rather than memorising
geometry-specific decisions.

Two further observations are worth naming. First, the spread in hybrid
RMSE across the table tracks the spread in PINN RMSE rather than the
spread in geometry: configurations on which the underlying PINN is
weaker (rows~1, 4, 5, 7) leave a larger residual hybrid RMSE because
the bound \eqref{eq:hybrid-bound} contains
$\|h-u^\star\|_{\widehat{\Omega}_P\cup\Gamma}$, which the rejector
controls only through partition choice and not through the PINN itself.
Second, the held-out RMSE ($2.27\!\times\!10^{-2}$) is on the better end
of the table, not the worse, despite being unseen during rejector
training. This is consistent with the PINN at the held-out triple being
of comparable quality to the best training PINNs, and with the rejector
having interpolated rather than extrapolated through the training
bracket. We do not claim the rejector would extend cleanly to triples
outside the bracket; that limitation, and the related question of
transfer to qualitatively different boundary conditions, are discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.
